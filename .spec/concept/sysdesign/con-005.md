## Overview

Defines the high-level architecture of the governance system as a stack of thirteen functional layers plus an anti-corruption architecture. Political authority originates from and remains with citizens; institutions facilitate governance, provide expertise, execute approved decisions, and protect rights, but do not hold decision-making authority themselves. Every public action moves through the same fixed lifecycle, and decision rights are kept structurally separate from execution rights.

## Key Points

- Core principle: political authority originates from and remains with citizens; institutions facilitate, execute, and protect rights but never own decision-making authority.
- System lifecycle: every public action follows the same sequence — problem identification, proposal creation, public review & expert analysis, budget & impact assessment, voting, implementation, public monitoring, outcome evaluation.

### Layer 1 — Citizen Identity
- Guarantees one person = one civic identity via verified identity, privacy protection, auditability, and anti-fraud protection.
- Citizen profile holds identity verification, residence, participation history, declared expertise, private voting history, and public contribution history.

### Layer 2 — Sphere of Impact
- Voting rights scale with impact rather than being universal per issue: street-level decisions vote by affected street, city by city, regional by region, national policy by the entire population.
- Reduces voter fatigue, uninformed participation, and political noise; increases relevance, accountability, and local ownership.

### Layers 3-4 — Problem Registry & Proposal Marketplace
- Any citizen can submit problems, opportunities, or improvement suggestions; each becomes publicly visible.
- Competing proposals for the same problem are evaluated side by side, rather than citizens voting yes/no on one government-issued proposal.

### Layer 5 — Expert Review
- Experts provide feasibility studies, risk analysis, cost estimates, and long-term projections; they cannot approve or block policy, and their opinions are public.
- Every proposal receives four scores: technical, economic, social impact, sustainability. Expert credibility is built through transparent contribution history (reputation), not institutional appointment. Citizens retain final authority.

### Layer 6 — Public Deliberation
- Structured discussion before voting, distinct from open social-media-style debate; arguments must link to evidence, studies, budgets, and impact reports.

### Layer 7 — Budget Layer
- No proposal enters voting without funding information: cost, funding source, maintenance cost, and expected benefits must be displayed; citizens vote on policy and spending together, preventing unfunded promises.
- Includes a real-time public ledger of tax inflow/outflow and project costs, with participatory allocation across categories such as education, infrastructure, healthcare, defense, research, and environment.

### Layer 8 — Voting Layer
- Decision types (local, regional, national, constitutional) carry different pass thresholds: simple majority for local projects, majority plus minimum participation for national infrastructure, supermajority for constitutional amendments.

### Layer 9 — Implementation Layer
- Professional civil servants execute approved decisions (execution, administration, logistics) but do not create policy; government becomes an implementation organization, not a decision monopoly.

### Layer 10 — Public Oversight Layer
- Every approved project gets a timeline, budget tracking, milestone reporting, and outcome metrics, observable by citizens in real time.

### Layer 11 — Civic Knowledge System
- Citizens receive educational materials, simulations, impact models, and expert explanations before voting on technical issues; goal is informed participation, not gatekeeping.
- Optional knowledge validation: citizens who review materials and pass comprehension checks on a technical issue can gain "informed voter" status before voting on it.

### Layer 12 — Delegated Expertise (optional)
- Citizens may temporarily delegate voting in specific domains (e.g. energy, healthcare, transportation, agriculture) to trusted specialists; delegation is voluntary, revocable, and issue-specific — a hybrid of direct and liquid democracy.

### Layer 13 — Constitutional Layer
- Certain rights (speech, due process, equality before law, property, human rights) cannot be overridden by ordinary voting; democracy governs policy, the constitution governs boundaries.

### Anti-Corruption Architecture
- Assumes corruption attempts will occur; counters with no hidden budgets, no anonymous lobbying, no permanent political class, no exclusive proposal access, no closed deliberation, and no untouchable institutions.

### Governance Formula
- People closest to a problem identify it; citizens and experts co-develop solutions; citizens directly decide priorities; institutions execute; society continuously evaluates outcomes. Power stays distributed, transparent, and accountable throughout.
- Decision rights and execution rights are kept structurally separate: citizens decide what should happen, professional institutions decide how to implement it efficiently — avoiding turning engineering, legal, or administrative detail into a public referendum.

Concrete technology and infrastructure decisions are formalized separately in ADR-015 through ADR-018 and ARCH-006 through ARCH-008, not in this concept document.
