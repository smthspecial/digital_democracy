## Overview

This document specifies the six operational subsystems that implement the Digital Democracy governance model: citizen identity, sphere of impact, competency and expertise, proposal/review/voting, decision formation under permanent disagreement, and system integrity/anti-capture design. Each subsystem translates a governance goal into concrete mechanics—verified identity, scope-based voting eligibility, non-transferable expertise, distributed civic duties, constraint-based decision-making, and layered accountability—while building in explicit defenses against capture, fraud, and power concentration. No module grants any single actor unilateral or irreversible authority; both governance rights and governance workload are deliberately distributed across the citizenry.

## Key Points

### Citizen Identity System

- One citizen holds exactly one civic identity linked to a verified legal identity; votes are cryptographically separated from identity, so eligibility is verified without exposing vote choice, and citizens can confirm their vote was counted without revealing its content.
- Identity creation is auditable and revocable only for death, loss of citizenship, or proven fraud; no single administrator can unilaterally disable a citizen's participation, and identity infrastructure sits under independent oversight rather than party control.

### Sphere of Impact System

- Every proposal receives a public, challengeable impact scope across six levels (property, street/neighborhood, municipality, regional, national, constitutional) that determines who may vote on it; scope disputes go to independent review bodies.
- Decision authority defaults to the smallest competent jurisdiction and escalates only when a problem exceeds that scope; jurisdiction boundaries are fixed, public, and independently reviewed, and boundary changes require public approval to block gerrymandering-style manipulation.

### Competency and Expertise System

- Competency is domain-specific and earned through four levels (General Citizen through Senior Domain Contributor) via an application, automated verification, public review, domain review, and approval pipeline; status expires periodically (2-5 years) and inactive experts lose active status.
- Competency grants enhanced visibility and advisory authority (proposal review, risk/impact assessment) only—never extra votes, vetoes, or binding power; claims remain continuously challengeable by any citizen, conflicts of interest must be disclosed, and reputation is tracked separately from competency.

### Proposal, Review, and Voting System

- Governance work—voting, proposal review, audit review, expertise verification, budget oversight—is distributed through randomized, rotating civic-duty assignments prioritized by sphere of impact, competency domain, then workload balancing and random selection; typical load is 2-4 hours per month.
- A proposal needs population-scaled public support (e.g., 50 neighborhood / 500 city / 50,000 national) before entering development, where competing alternatives, cost estimates, and modifications may coexist; voters receive a package with problem statement, proposed solution, budget impact, expert analysis, and alternatives.
- Non-participation escalates through reminder, reduced assignments, then temporary inactive status without loss of constitutional protections; valid exemptions (illness, disability, military service, caregiving) pause obligations penalty-free, and delegation of duties is voluntary, transparent, revocable, and auto-expiring; reviewer, auditor, expert, and delegate roles all rotate rather than becoming permanent.

### Decision Formation and Collective Agreement

- The system assumes disagreement is permanent and does not require consensus; it uses constraint-based governance (agree on constraints before debating solutions), separates preference declaration from argument, and generates multiple structured alternatives rather than a single "correct" proposal.
- Multi-option voting (ranked choice, approval, preference scoring) replaces binary yes/no framing to reduce polarization; budgets are partitioned into categories so priorities compete within pools rather than as one zero-sum whole; AI may assist in summarizing preferences and generating alternatives but holds no decision authority.
- Decision-making effort scales with impact, from local review/vote for minor issues up to expert assessment, extended deliberation, and supermajority approval for constitutional change; outcomes are measured against promised results after implementation, and unresolved deadlocks proceed through a fixed eight-stage escalation ending in a final decision procedure so nothing stays blocked indefinitely.

### System Integrity, Authority, and Anti-Capture Design

- Resolves the Authority Paradox (a final authority can be captured; no authority is unstable) with four independent, non-overlapping layers: Protocol (rules, changeable only via supermajority, delay, and audit confirmation), Implementation (operators execute only approved actions), Audit (observes and reports only), and Citizen (retains final policy authority); no single layer can modify, execute, and validate an action.
- Anti-capture mechanisms: immutable append-only public action logs, multi-approval requirements for critical changes, rotating randomized oversight and audit roles, mandatory conflict-of-interest disclosure and exclusion, standardized non-discretionary compensation, delayed execution windows for high-impact actions, and redundant independent auditing bodies.
- Core principle: power is decomposed rather than eliminated—every powerful action requires observable, costly-to-fake coordination across independent actors, and no single entity or aligned group can execute an irreversible governance action alone.
