## Overview

Defines the seven principles governing how digital democracy allocates decision authority, money, and information. Together they push authority toward those affected, separate issues instead of bundling them into party politics, and replace periodic elections with continuous, transparent, informed participation. Each principle in the source is paired with a worked example illustrating the shift from representative-democracy practice to the proposed model.

## Key Points

### Principle 1: Decisions Belong to Those Affected by Them

- Influence over a decision should scale with proximity to its impact: local residents, transit users, and those affected by environmental policy understand their domains better than distant institutions.
- Governance should begin at the level of impact rather than being centralized by default.
- Example: redesigning a public square — under representative democracy citizens have little direct influence beyond electing representatives; under digital democracy, affected residents review proposals, submit alternatives, see costs, and vote directly.

### Principle 2: Public Money Belongs to the Public

- Public budgets originate entirely from citizens (taxation, fees, public assets), so citizens should have continuous visibility into allocation.
- Transparency alone is insufficient; citizens should also have meaningful influence over spending priorities.
- Example: a municipality's annual budget is prioritized by residents allocating preferences across categories (roads, transit, education, parks, housing, culture) instead of being decided internally by officials.

### Principle 3: Governance Should Be Issue-Based

- Elections bundle hundreds of unrelated issues into one vote, forcing citizens into compromises no party fully represents, before governance even starts.
- Digital democracy separates issues so citizens evaluate individual proposals independently.
- Example: instead of one party vote every four years, citizens vote separately on transportation, healthcare, education, and taxation proposals, each judged on its own merits.

### Principle 4: Participation Requires Responsibility

- Democracy works best when participants understand what they're deciding; complex issues require technical knowledge.
- The goal is not to restrict participation but to increase decision quality through informed participation.
- Example: before voting on nuclear energy policy, citizens receive neutral educational material, multi-perspective expert arguments, and economic/environmental impact assessments — participation as learning, not reaction.

### Principle 5: Expertise Should Inform, Not Rule

- Expertise alone should not grant political authority, and public opinion alone should not replace expertise; effective governance combines both.
- Experts provide information; citizens provide legitimacy.
- Example: engineers determine which bridge designs are technically feasible ("can this be done?"); citizens choose among them based on community priorities and budget ("should this be done?").

### Principle 6: Governance Should Be Continuous

- Elections occur every few years, but problems emerge daily, leaving citizens with little influence during the gap.
- Digital democracy replaces the periodic-event model with continuous, ongoing participation.
- Example: a traffic congestion proposal moves from submission through public discussion, published cost estimates, digital voting, and implementation in months rather than years.

### Principle 7: Transparency Is the Default

- Every significant decision should be traceable: what was proposed, who supported it, cost, implementation progress, and whether objectives were achieved.
- Opacity enables corruption, waste, and mistrust; transparency creates accountability.
- Example: a school construction project's budget, contractors, timeline, cost changes, and completion status remain continuously observable to citizens, replacing after-the-fact investigation.
